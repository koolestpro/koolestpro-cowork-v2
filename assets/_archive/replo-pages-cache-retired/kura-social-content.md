## LI-PDP — PDP Cover Video

Before there was a homepage, a listicle, or an advertorial, there was one product page.

I started this portfolio with the hardest page to build well: the product page. It's the page that carries the most weight. It has to introduce the brand, agitate the problem, present the solution, prove it works, kill every objection, and ask for the sale, all without losing the reader halfway down.

KURA's PDP follows a villain/hero structure. The "villain" section names the problem most people never think about: plastic boards that harbor bacteria, wood boards that crack and stain. Then the "hero" section introduces KURA as the upgrade, using 304 stainless steel that's non-porous and dishwasher safe.

From there, a before/after comparison, a 3-step how-it-works block, review cards, three circular trust-stat indicators, a head-to-head comparison table against generic boards, an 8-question FAQ accordion, three trust pillars, and an email capture. Thirteen sections in total, each one a deliberate handoff to the next.

The buy box is sticky on desktop. Star rating sits above the product title. Price is impossible to miss. None of that is decoration. It's conversion architecture.

This page became the template the rest of the portfolio builds from. Every homepage, listicle, and advertorial after it inherited its color system, its type scale, and its standard for what "done" looks like.

Built in HTML/CSS/vanilla JS. No page builder. No theme.

## LI-HP — Homepage Cover Video

Built an entire brand from nothing.

No brief. No identity. No product name. Just a dropship stainless steel cutting board on an AliExpress supplier page.

I named it KURA. I positioned it for home cooks who take kitchen hygiene seriously. I wrote the tagline ("Clean Surface. Clear Mind."), chose the palette (deep slate, warm cream, burnished copper), and hand-picked a three-font typographic system — display, serif, body — before writing a single line of HTML.

Then I built the homepage.

The experience opens with a preloader that types the brand name in. Then the site reveals. GSAP-powered horizontal scroll for the use-case section. Clip-path reveals on brand imagery. A parallax hero. Marquee trust bar on pure CSS. Scroll-triggered fade-ins on every section via IntersectionObserver. Zero external JS libraries.

The goal wasn't "nice website." The goal was to make a product that retails for $48 feel like it belongs on the shelf next to brands spending millions on brand equity.

That gap between what a product costs to source and what a customer is willing to pay for it — that gap is design. It's copy. It's how fast the page loads. It's whether the above-the-fold gives them a reason to keep reading.

This homepage is part of an ongoing portfolio of Replo-quality pages built from scratch to prove that point.

Built in HTML / CSS / vanilla JS. No frameworks. No templates.

## LI-LI — Listicle Cover Video

The listicle is one of the most underused pages in eCommerce funnels.

Most brands don't build them. The ones that do usually hand them off to a content writer who treats it like a blog post — no CTA flow, no before/after, no conversion architecture.

A listicle done properly does two things at once:

First: it earns organic search traffic on problem-aware queries ("why does my cutting board smell", "is my cutting board making me sick"). These are people who know they have a problem but don't have a solution yet. They're searching with intent.

Second: it moves those people from awareness to purchase without feeling like a sales pitch. They're reading an article. The article happens to introduce your product as the answer to the exact problem they searched for.

This is the KURA listicle — built on my li-03 template. The headline: "5 Problems Your Cutting Board Is Causing Right Now."

Each problem block follows the same structure: agitate the pain, show what changes, introduce the mechanism. Then the page pivots. Transformation strip. Social proof. Final CTA.

Two-column alternating layout on desktop. Full single-column collapse on mobile. Inline CTAs at the highest-friction moments. Review cards from real-feeling customers.

Editorial at the top. Conversion funnel at the bottom.

If your store is running paid traffic to a product page and wondering why it doesn't convert — this is the page you're missing.

## LI-AD — Advertorial Cover Video

Cold traffic lands differently.

Someone who finds your product page from an ad already has their guard up. They know they're being sold to. The headline is a pitch. The bullet points are claims. The reviews feel placed.

An advertorial changes the dynamic entirely.

Instead of saying "buy this," it says "let me show you something you didn't know about your kitchen." Instead of a product headline, there's a publication masthead. Instead of benefit bullets, there are paragraphs written in editorial voice — with expert citations, reader case studies, and a sticky product rail that travels alongside the article quietly.

By the time the reader reaches the CTA, they think they've reached their own conclusion.

For KURA, I built the full editorial treatment — published under a fictional site called The Kitchen Standard. Article: "The One Item In Your Kitchen That's Making You Sick."

The structure follows the advertorial framework precisely: opening hook → problem deep dive → discovery moment → product introduction (natural, not forced) → mechanism explanation → expert authority quote → two customer transformation stories → mid-article CTA → objection handling Q&A → final close with urgency → five reader comments for bottom-of-page social proof.

Every section earns the next. Nothing is filler.

This is the page type that makes paid traffic scale — particularly for products where customers don't know they need a solution yet.

Built in pure HTML/CSS/JS. Two-column editorial layout. Sticky rail on desktop. Full mobile collapse. Zero frameworks.

## LI-PP — Post-Purchase Cover Video

Everyone optimizes the page before checkout. Almost nobody optimizes the page after it.

Once a customer pays, most stores hand them a default confirmation screen and move on. That's a mistake. The five seconds right after checkout are the highest-trust moment in the entire funnel. The customer just proved intent with their card. Nothing you show them next is intrusive. It's expected.

For KURA, I built the post-purchase page to do three jobs at once.

First, it confirms the order in a way that feels like a brand moment, not a receipt. Dark background, a single clean checkmark, the order summary laid out simply.

Second, it makes a one-time offer: 30% off a second board size, with a live countdown timer and a genuine one-click add (no new checkout flow). This is the single highest-converting upsell placement in commerce, and most stores never use it.

Third, it prevents buyer's remorse before it starts. A 3-step "what happens next" block removes anxiety about shipping. A social proof section reinforces the decision. A care guide sets expectations early instead of generating a support ticket in week two. And a community email capture with share buttons turns a single sale into the start of a relationship.

Post-purchase pages are the most overlooked page type in eCommerce. They're also some of the cheapest revenue on the table, since the traffic is already paid for.

Built in HTML/CSS/vanilla JS. Countdown timer, click feedback, and scroll animations all hand-coded, zero libraries.
